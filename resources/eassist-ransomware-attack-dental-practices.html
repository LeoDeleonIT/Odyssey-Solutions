eAssist Ransomware Attack Claim: What Dental Practices Should Do Now

eAssist has confirmed an investigation into a potential information security incident. A ransomware group has made a separate, unverified claim. Dental practices can tighten vendor access now without treating unknowns as facts.

Published September 14, 20269-minute readFact-checked September 14, 2026Published by Odyssey Solutions · Dental cybersecurity guidance for Greater Houston practices.

On September 6, 2026, the DireWolf ransomware group listed eAssist Dental Solutions on its extortion site. On September 8, eAssist notified customers that it was investigating a potential information security incident and had engaged outside experts, according to an advisory from the Georgia Dental Association.

The distinction is important. eAssist confirmed an investigation, not the ransomware group's allegations. In the September 8 notification summarized by the association, eAssist had not confirmed that customer or patient information was accessed or compromised. No specific records, credentials, or patient-data types had been verified as exposed. Odyssey found no authoritative public update changing that status in a review through September 14.

Status as of September 14: the investigation and precautionary customer guidance are confirmed. The ransomware attribution, alleged system access, data theft, affected practices, and affected patients remain unverified in the public record.

What is known and what is not

  • Confirmed: eAssist told customers it is investigating a potential information security incident with help from outside experts.
  • Confirmed: eAssist advised customers to change relevant system passwords and remove old or inactive accounts as a precaution.
  • Reported: DireWolf listed eAssist on an extortion site and claimed access to internal systems.
  • Not publicly confirmed: whether ransomware was deployed, whether information was taken, which practices or patients may be affected, or which data types may be involved.

A criminal group's leak-site post is not a forensic report, a regulator notice, or a vendor breach notification. It is still a meaningful signal. The right response is to reduce your practice's exposure while preserving the evidence needed for whatever eAssist's investigation finds.

What an eAssist customer should do today

1. Open an internal incident record

Save the eAssist notice and every follow-up. Record when your practice received it, who is coordinating the response, which systems eAssist could access, and each action your team takes. Keep patient information out of general support tickets and public communication channels.

2. Map every account and connection eAssist used

Do not stop at the eAssist portal. Inventory access to your practice-management software, clearinghouse, claims tools, insurance carrier portals, remote-access platform, document exchange, and any integration tokens or API connections. Record whether each path uses a named user, a shared login, a service account, or a saved credential.

3. Rotate credentials and terminate old sessions

Change credentials for accounts eAssist used or could access. Use new, unique passwords rather than variations of the old ones. Then revoke active sessions, saved browser sessions, application passwords, tokens, and connections where the platform allows it. A password change does not always end every existing session.

4. Disable stale accounts and reduce privilege

Disable old, duplicate, and inactive vendor accounts. Replace shared logins with named accounts where the system supports them, and limit each account to the access required for current work. Coordinate changes with practice leadership so urgent billing work is not interrupted without a continuity plan.

5. Require multifactor authentication

Turn on MFA wherever it is available, with priority given to email, remote access, administrator accounts, clearinghouses, payer portals, and financial systems. CISA's ransomware guidance specifically recommends phishing-resistant MFA for critical services and least-privilege access for third parties.

6. Preserve and review activity logs

Before logs expire, preserve sign-in and activity records for relevant systems. Review them for unfamiliar users, devices, locations, exports, account creation, privilege changes, token creation, or remote sessions. If you find suspicious activity, preserve the original evidence and involve your IT, security, insurance, and legal response contacts before making broad system changes.

7. Warn staff about convincing follow-on scams

Tell billing, front-desk, and management staff to expect messages about password resets, claim corrections, portal access, payment changes, or urgent IT help. Verify unusual requests using a known phone number or an established vendor channel, not contact information inside the unexpected message.

8. Ask eAssist for a written, practice-specific update

Use an established eAssist contact channel and ask concise questions. Is your practice in scope? Which systems and time periods are being investigated? Were practice credentials, tokens, or remote-access paths involved? Is patient information in scope? What containment steps have been completed? What actions should your practice take now, and when will the next update arrive?

9. Review your BAA, contracts, and cyber policy

Locate the applicable business associate agreement and service contract. Confirm the incident-reporting timeline, security contact, cooperation requirements, evidence obligations, and responsibility for notices. HHS explains that BAAs must require business associates to report security incidents and breaches of unsecured PHI, while the agreement may set requirements that are more specific or faster than the federal outer limit. Involve qualified HIPAA counsel and your cyber insurer based on your facts and policy terms.

10. Prepare a billing continuity plan

Identify time-sensitive claims, insurance verifications, payment postings, appeals, and follow-up work that could be delayed. Assign an internal owner, document approved alternate workflows, and maintain a reconciliation list so work completed during an interruption can be checked when normal service resumes.

Does this mean your practice has a reportable HIPAA breach?

Not based on the public facts alone. An investigation into a potential security incident and a criminal group's claim do not establish that unsecured PHI was accessed, acquired, used, or disclosed. The Georgia Dental Association advisory specifically cautions practices not to assume a reportable HIPAA breach has occurred while the investigation continues.

If later findings establish an impermissible use or disclosure of unsecured PHI, the HHS Breach Notification Rule and the practice's agreements will guide the risk assessment, documentation, and notices. A business associate must notify the covered entity after discovering a breach of unsecured PHI. The covered entity remains responsible for ensuring required individual notices occur, although responsibilities can be allocated by agreement. Do not make a patient-notification decision from a leak-site claim alone; involve qualified privacy or legal counsel.

What not to do

  • Do not repeat alleged data categories or victim counts as confirmed facts.
  • Do not visit criminal leak sites or download alleged data to investigate the claim yourself.
  • Do not use the same replacement password across multiple vendor systems.
  • Do not delete logs or account history when disabling access.
  • Do not wait for a final investigation report before inventorying and tightening vendor access.
  • Do not send patient information, credentials, or screenshots containing sensitive data through a public website form.

The broader lesson for every dental practice

Even practices that do not use eAssist should run the same exercise for billing companies, consultants, software vendors, IT providers, imaging support, and remote staff. A vendor can sit outside your office while holding access to the systems that run claims, payments, scheduling, or patient records.

CISA recommends considering the security practices of third parties, limiting them to the devices and systems required for their role, and applying least privilege. A current vendor-access register, named accounts, MFA, documented offboarding, and tested downtime procedures make any future incident easier to contain and explain.

eAssist incident FAQ

Has eAssist confirmed a ransomware attack?

No authoritative public source reviewed by Odyssey through September 14 confirms a ransomware attack. eAssist has confirmed that it is investigating a potential information security incident. DireWolf's ransomware claim is separate and remains unverified.

Was patient information stolen?

That has not been publicly confirmed. The September 8 customer notice, as summarized by the Georgia Dental Association, said eAssist was still determining the nature and scope of the incident and whether customer or patient information was affected.

Is changing passwords enough?

No. Password changes are important, but practices should also revoke sessions and tokens, disable stale accounts, review logs, enable MFA, reduce privileges, and verify remote-access paths.

Should a practice stop using eAssist?

There is no universal answer in the public facts. A practice can reduce access, ask for a written update, and prepare continuity options while leadership evaluates service needs, contract terms, security findings, and risk with its advisers.

Should patients be notified now?

Not solely because of the public ransomware claim. Notification depends on verified facts, a HIPAA breach assessment, applicable law, and the practice's agreements. Seek qualified legal or privacy guidance for your organization.
