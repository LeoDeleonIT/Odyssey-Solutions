Employee Left? Remove Access From Business Accounts

A departing employee does not mean there is a security problem. It does mean the office should know which accounts, devices, files, and services need a clean handoff.

Published September 26, 20263-minute readBusiness IT guidePublished by Odyssey Solutions · Guidance for Greater Houston businesses.

When someone leaves, the goal is simple: keep the business running while making sure access matches the person’s current role. A rushed change can lock out the wrong team member, lose a customer conversation, or leave a former employee connected to an account nobody owns.

Use one owner: have a manager or designated operations person confirm the last working day, the accounts involved, and who receives the work before access changes are made.

This is the short handoff for a departure. For hiring, role changes, and a complete repeatable process, use the full onboarding and offboarding guide.

1. Make a short list before the last day

List the business email account, shared mailboxes, cloud files, phone or messaging apps, workstations, remote-access tools, accounting or practice software, vendor portals, and administrator accounts the person uses. Ask the supervisor which customer work, approvals, or recurring tasks must move to another owner.

2. Hand off work without sharing passwords

Move mailbox access, files, calendars, customer contacts, and service ownership through the platform’s normal permissions. Do not solve a handoff by sending a password in email or text. Individual accounts make it easier to see who has access and to remove it when the role ends.

Before deleting an account, removing its license, or wiping a device, confirm which business records must be retained, who receives the work, and how the platform preserves that data. Blocking sign-in and deleting an account are different actions. Microsoft’s former-employee checklist separates access removal from preserving and transferring business information.

3. Remove access on the agreed date

Block the departing employee’s sign-in and end active sessions at the authorized handoff time. Follow the business policy and applicable agreements. Review multifactor authentication methods, recovery email addresses, authenticator devices, saved browser sessions, vendor portals, and remote-control tools. Collect company devices and confirm who will manage the account after the transition.

4. Check the overlooked places

Look for shared passwords, browser-based access, phone apps, group memberships, forwarding rules, backups, copier portals, security-camera apps, and software vendors with named support contacts. If a vendor has remote access, update the access record and remove any temporary credential that no longer has a business purpose.

5. Keep a simple record

Record the date, account or device, action taken, person who confirmed it, and any follow-up owner. That record helps when a former mailbox needs to be searched, a customer email must be recovered, or a new employee needs the same access later.
