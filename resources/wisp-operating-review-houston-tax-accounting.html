Is Your WISP Still Usable? A Review for Houston Tax and Accounting Firms

Review the owner, data scope, vendor responsibilities and operating evidence behind your written information security plan.

Published October 1, 20264-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions · Guidance for Greater Houston businesses.

Start with the plan you actually use

A written information security plan is useful when the people named in it can explain how the work happens today. For a Houston tax or accounting office, this review connects the document to current ownership and evidence rather than adding another generic tax-season checklist.

The IRS June 16, 2026 tax tip reminds tax professionals to tailor a WISP to their business and regularly review it as operations change. This is a reminder about maintaining a plan, not an announcement of a new law.

The FTC Safeguards Rule guidance explains requirements for covered financial institutions, including tax-preparation firms. Confirm applicability and any exceptions with the appropriate adviser. This article is an operational review aid, not a determination of your obligations.

Confirm the owner and decision path

Find the person currently responsible for the information security program. Ask who approves changes, who receives unresolved issues and who acts when that person is unavailable. If the document names a former employee or an old provider, record the gap and assign the update.

A practical completion check is that the responsible person can locate the current plan, describe their role and identify the next unresolved action. Hiring an IT provider does not remove the firm’s own responsibility for decisions.

Match the plan to current data and systems

Compare the plan with the places your team now collects, stores and shares client information. Include paper records, email, portals, tax software, cloud folders and approved remote-work arrangements. Record changes since the last review, including offices, staff, applications and providers.

Use a system inventory and record categories in the review. Do not copy client returns or identity details into a general action list. The reviewer needs to see where evidence is held and who can access it.

Ask for evidence of operating work

Choose a few activities from your own plan and ask for their latest completion record. Examples of useful review questions:

  • Who last reviewed access for staff who changed roles or left?
  • Where is the latest recovery test result and its unresolved follow-up?
  • Can staff locate the current incident contact and reporting instructions?
  • Which provider reviews are due, and who owns them?
  • What changed after the last risk review?

These questions are Odyssey’s suggested way to make a discussion concrete. They do not replace required assessments, testing, reporting or legal advice. A policy document and an operating record answer different questions.

Review provider responsibilities

Check whether current agreements and working arrangements identify who handles access, support, incident escalation and relevant safeguards. Record any unclear responsibility as an open item rather than assuming the other party handles it.

The FTC guidance addresses service-provider oversight and keeping the security program current. Review the official guidance with the person responsible for your program before treating any local checklist as complete.

End with a short decision record

For each gap, record the issue, owner, next action, target review date and evidence needed to close it. For example, a fictional firm might find that its plan still names an old portal provider. The owner would confirm the replacement arrangement, update the plan and record the completed review.

Schedule the next review around changes and applicable requirements. A calendar reminder is useful only when someone owns the work. For broader equipment, accounts and support preparation, see the tax-office IT readiness checklist.
