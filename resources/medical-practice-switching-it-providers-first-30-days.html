Switching IT Providers at a Medical Practice: The First 30 Days

A first-30-days plan for medical practices changing IT providers: BAAs, admin access, EHR vendor contacts, backup restore tests and a security baseline.

Published November 11, 20265-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

Most medical practices change IT providers because something has gone wrong: slow responses, repeat outages, or nobody able to answer a basic question about backups. The change itself is a risky period. Access is moving between companies, monitoring may lapse, and the EHR vendor may not know who to talk to.

This guide covers the first 30 days with a new IT provider from the practice's side: what the practice should own, what the new provider should deliver, and how to tell whether onboarding is going well. For the exit side of the change, use our IT provider handover checklist.

Before day one: settle ownership and paperwork

Three things should be in place before the new provider touches your systems.

A signed agreement with clear scope. You should know what is covered, what is billed separately and how to request help.

A business associate agreement. An IT provider that can access systems holding patient information generally creates, receives, maintains or transmits that information on the practice's behalf, which makes it a business associate under HIPAA. HHS explains that a covered entity needs satisfactory written assurances, a business associate agreement, before sharing protected health information with one. HHS: Business Associates Your compliance adviser should review the agreement.

Practice-owned admin access. CISA's guidance for customers of managed service providers says organizations can outsource IT work but cannot fully hand off IT responsibility, and should keep control of access. CISA: Risk Considerations for MSP Customers In practice, that means the practice holds, or can recover, the top-level accounts for Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, the domain registrar, the firewall and key vendor portals. Store those credentials somewhere secure that does not depend on any one provider.

Week 1: discovery and secure access

The first week is about learning what exists and getting safe access to it. A good provider will:

  • Inventory computers, servers, network equipment, printers and mobile devices
  • List every administrator account and who it belongs to
  • Install its own monitoring and remote support tools with named accounts and multifactor authentication
  • Confirm what the outgoing provider's tools and accounts are, so they can be removed on a set date

That last step matters. CISA's joint advisory on managed service provider threats notes that disabling provider accounts when a contract ends is easy to overlook. CISA: Protecting Against Cyber Threats to MSPs and their Customers Old remote access agents and admin accounts left behind are a real exposure.

Ask for a short written summary at the end of week one: what was found, what is urgent, and what is still unknown.

Week 1 to 2: EHR and vendor contacts

Medical practices depend on vendors the IT provider has to work with: the EHR or practice-management company, clearinghouse, lab interfaces, e-prescribing, imaging or diagnostic equipment, the internet provider and the phone provider. Many of these vendors only talk to authorized contacts.

During the first two weeks:

  • Give the new provider a vendor list with account numbers and support contacts
  • Tell each vendor who the new IT contact is, and update any authorized-contact list
  • Remove the old provider from those lists on the agreed date
  • Confirm which vendors host data in the cloud and which rely on an on-site server

HHS's cloud computing guidance explains that a cloud provider that stores or processes patient information for a practice is generally a business associate, even if it cannot view the data. HHS: Guidance on HIPAA & Cloud Computing That makes this vendor review a good moment to confirm your agreements are current. Week 2: verify backups with a restore

Do not accept "backups are running" during a transition. Ask the new provider to confirm what is backed up, where the copies are, how long they are kept and whether any copy is isolated from the main network. Then ask for a test restore of a real file or system to a safe location, with the result written down.

If the outgoing provider owned the backup service, plan the cutover so there is never a gap with no backup at all. Keep the old backups available until the new ones are confirmed.

Week 3: set a security baseline

Once access and backups are stable, the provider should report on:

  • Multifactor authentication for email, remote access and admin accounts
  • Operating system and application update status
  • Endpoint protection coverage
  • Unsupported computers or servers that need a replacement plan
  • Former employees or vendors whose accounts are still active

HHS's 405(d) program publishes Health Industry Cybersecurity Practices, with a technical volume aimed at small healthcare organizations. It is a practical reference for what to fix first. HHS 405(d): Health Industry Cybersecurity Practices Ask the provider to rank findings by patient and business impact, not just by technical severity.

Week 4: staff, documentation and a 90-day plan

By the end of the first month, staff should know exactly how to request help, what counts as urgent and what number to call after hours. Send a short note to the whole team and post it at the front desk.

The provider should hand over starting documentation: a device list, network overview, vendor contacts, admin account owners and backup details. This record belongs to the practice.

Finish with a review meeting. Go through what was found, what was fixed, what still needs a decision and what the next 90 days look like. Our Healthcare IT Readiness Review produces a similar written ownership map and 90-day plan.

Signs onboarding is going well

  • You received written findings, not just "everything looks fine"
  • The old provider's access has been removed on a known date
  • A test restore was completed and recorded
  • Your EHR and other key vendors know who to call
  • Staff know how to get help
  • The practice still holds its own top-level admin access

Common mistakes

Changing everything at once is the most common one. Swapping the firewall, email platform and backup system in the same week makes any problem hard to trace. Stabilize first, then improve in planned steps outside patient hours. The second is ending the old contract before access, backups and documentation have been confirmed.
