Google Calendar Ownership Is Changing: Protect Shared Work Calendars

Google's October 2026 calendar change affects shared calendars when an owner leaves. Check ownership, transfer completion and staff access before account changes.

Published October 12, 20264-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

A shared work calendar can become essential long after everyone forgets who created it. Staff might use it for project deadlines, equipment reservations or office coverage while its owner moves into another role. Google's latest calendar update makes that ownership detail worth checking now.

On October 5, 2026, Google announced the rollout of changes tying secondary calendars to their owners for non-personal Workspace accounts. Deleting an owner also deletes their secondary calendars; suspending an owner suspends those calendars. Google says ownership should be transferred before either action. The announcement describes an extended rollout, potentially longer than 15 days, so October 5 is a rollout start rather than proof that every organization changed that day. Read Google's announcement.

For a business using these calendars, the practical task is to identify the owner, agree on a successor and verify that the handover finished. Put that check into planned staff changes before a calendar becomes unavailable.

Find the calendars your team actually uses

Start with the business workflow. Ask office managers and team leads which calendars people rely on each week. Include calendars used by several locations, calendars created for an old project that is still running, and ones with names that sound like departments rather than people.

For each important calendar, record:

  • Its name and business purpose
  • The account that owns it
  • The person responsible for its accuracy
  • The staff or groups who need access
  • The proposed successor if its owner changes roles
  • Any booking tool, website or workflow that depends on it

Keep the inventory in an approved business location. Calendar names and events can reveal confidential activities, so collect only the detail needed for this review. A list of owners and dependencies is usually more useful than copying all the event contents.

Confirm ownership separately from sharing permissions

A calendar may be widely shared while remaining tied to one person's account. Google's March 30 explanation of the transition distinguishes ownership from high-level sharing permissions. It also says organizational calendar ownership must remain within the organization. An external collaborator's editing access does not make them the appropriate successor. See Google's earlier lifecycle explanation.

Ask the administrator to verify the actual owner instead of relying on who can currently edit events. Choose a successor who understands the calendar's purpose and has an appropriate managed account. Avoid solving an ownership gap by sharing someone's password or creating an unmanaged personal account.

Consider a fictional Houston service company whose dispatch calendar was created by a former office manager. Several employees can edit appointments. The review should establish which account owns the calendar and who should take responsibility next. The fact that dispatch still works today does not answer those questions.

Choose the transfer route and confirm completion

Google provides owner-initiated transfers in Calendar settings and administrator tools for transferring secondary calendars. These workflows have different confirmation behavior. In the user-initiated process, Google says the recipient receives an email and has 60 days to accept; the original owner remains the owner until acceptance. Sending the invitation alone therefore does not complete the transfer. Review the owner transfer instructions.

Have the authorized administrator choose the appropriate method and scope. Google's administration guide separates secondary-calendar transfers from transfers of a user's primary-calendar events. Its secondary-calendar tool can transfer all of an undeleted user's secondary calendars, so the administrator should understand which calendars are included before proceeding. Transfers can take time. Use Google's administrator guidance.

Set a completion check that names the calendar, confirmed new owner and verification date. If acceptance or processing remains pending, leave the handover open with a responsible person. Do not treat a submitted request or an email notification as evidence that ownership changed.

Test the work after the transfer

Ask a normal staff member to confirm that they can view the right calendar and perform their usual authorized work. Have the new owner check the settings and an upcoming event. Where the calendar feeds another system, ask that system's owner to test the relevant connection too.

Use a harmless test event if needed, with no confidential customer information or unnecessary invitations. Record what was checked and any problem found. If staff lose access, investigate the permissions with the administrator rather than broadly sharing the calendar to make the immediate error disappear.

Plan this review ahead of routine departures. If an account must be contained immediately for security reasons, follow the incident-response process and let an authorized administrator coordinate preservation. Calendar housekeeping should never require leaving unsafe account access active.

Give calendar ownership a lasting place in operations

Add one clear question to planned account changes: “Which shared secondary calendars does this person own, and has their transfer been verified?” Repeat the review when an employee changes teams or a project changes hands, even when no account deletion is planned.
