How to Prepare for a HIPAA Security Risk Assessment: The Technical Side

Gather the IT evidence a HIPAA security risk assessment needs: where patient data lives, device and account lists, network diagrams, backups and safeguards.

Published November 6, 20265-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

A HIPAA security risk assessment goes faster and produces better results when the practice walks in with its technical facts already gathered. Without them, much of the time goes to answering "we think so" questions, and the final report fills up with unknowns instead of decisions.

This guide is for medical and dental practice owners and managers in Texas who have an assessment coming up, whether they run it internally, use the federal SRA Tool, or work with an outside assessor. It covers the IT evidence to collect beforehand. It is not legal advice, and no checklist or assessment guarantees HIPAA compliance. For the assessment process itself, see our guide to HIPAA risk analysis for Texas dental practices.

What HHS expects from the assessment

The HIPAA Security Rule requires covered entities and business associates to conduct "an accurate and thorough assessment" of risks to electronic protected health information. HHS says the rule does not prescribe a specific method, because methods vary with the size and complexity of the organization. HHS: Guidance on Risk Analysis

For small and medium practices, the HHS Security Risk Assessment Tool is a free starting point. It is currently at version 3.7. HHS notes that using it is not required and does not guarantee compliance, and that the Windows version stores answers locally. HealthIT.gov: Security Risk Assessment Tool NIST SP 800-66 Revision 2 is the more detailed federal resource for mapping Security Rule requirements to cybersecurity practices. NIST SP 800-66 Rev. 2

HHS has also proposed changes to the Security Rule. Its summary page states that the rule currently in effect still applies while the proposal is pending. HHS: Summary of the HIPAA Security Rule Ask your compliance adviser how the proposal affects your planning.

1. Map where patient information lives

Start with a list of every place electronic patient information is created, received, stored or sent. Common entries include:

  • The EHR or practice-management system, and where its database runs (on-site server or vendor cloud)
  • Imaging software and image storage
  • Email, including shared mailboxes and any encrypted email service
  • Scanners, copiers and fax services
  • Patient communication, scheduling, payment and review platforms
  • Backups, on-site and off-site
  • Laptops, tablets and phones used for work
  • Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace file storage

For each entry, note who the vendor is and whether a business associate agreement is on file.

2. Bring an accurate device and account inventory

The assessor will ask what devices and accounts exist. Prepare:

  • A device list with type, location, operating system, owner and whether the disk is encrypted
  • Any computers running unsupported operating systems, such as Windows 10 machines without Extended Security Updates
  • A user account list for the EHR, email and the network, with each person's role
  • A list of administrator accounts and who controls each one
  • Accounts for former employees or vendors that should no longer exist

Account lists are where many practices find their first easy fixes.

3. Document the network and remote access

A simple diagram helps: internet connections, firewall, switches, Wi-Fi networks and how guest Wi-Fi is separated. Then list every way someone can reach practice systems from outside the building: VPN, remote desktop tools, vendor support tools and cloud portals. Note whether each requires multifactor authentication.

Vendor remote access deserves its own line. Record which vendors can connect, how, and how their access is approved and logged.

4. Gather evidence for the safeguards you already have

Assessments look for evidence, not intentions. Collect current examples of:

  • Update and patch status for computers and servers
  • Endpoint protection coverage and recent alert handling
  • Backup job reports and the date and result of the most recent restore test
  • Multifactor authentication status for email and remote access
  • Security awareness training records
  • Any security incidents or near misses since the last assessment, and what was done

HHS's voluntary Cybersecurity Performance Goals for healthcare are a practical reference for which safeguards to show evidence for first. HHS: HPH Cybersecurity Performance Goals

5. Check physical safeguards

Walk the office before the assessment. Where is the server or network equipment? Is it locked? Can patients see screens at the front desk or in operatories? Are retired computers and drives stored securely until they are wiped? Photos of the server area and network closet help an outside assessor.

6. Find last year's report and remediation list

If you have a previous assessment, bring it along with the list of items it recommended. Mark which were completed, which are in progress and which were accepted as risks. An assessment that repeats last year's findings without progress is a signal worth discussing with leadership.

Who should be involved

Plan for the practice owner or administrator, the person responsible for privacy and security, the office manager, and your IT provider. Your EHR or practice-management vendor may need to answer questions about what they host and secure. The assessor or compliance adviser makes the risk determinations. IT supplies the technical facts.

A note on Texas

Texas has its own medical records privacy law, Chapter 181 of the Texas Health and Safety Code, which can apply to more organizations than HIPAA alone. Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 181 Ask a qualified legal or compliance adviser how it applies to your practice. The technical preparation in this guide supports both conversations.

After the assessment

The assessment is the start, not the finish. HHS describes risk analysis as an ongoing process, followed by a plan to reduce the risks found. Turn the findings into a dated list with an owner for each item, and keep the evidence you gathered so next year starts faster.
