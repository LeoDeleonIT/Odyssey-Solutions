Business Email Hacked? What to Do First

If a mailbox is sending strange messages, has a new forwarding rule, or shows sign-ins you do not recognize, treat it as an account problem that needs a clear owner and a careful response.

Published September 26, 20263-minute readBusiness IT guidePublished by Odyssey Solutions · Guidance for Greater Houston businesses.

A business email account often handles invoices, customer questions, resets, shared files, and vendor requests. If something looks wrong, do not rush to erase messages or reset every account. Start by protecting the affected account, writing down what you saw, and getting the right people involved.

Start here: use a known administrator or support contact to secure the account. Do not grant remote access to an unexpected caller who says they can fix it.

1. Confirm what looks wrong

Record the mailbox, time, device, and activity that caused concern. Examples include unfamiliar sign-ins, password-reset notices you did not request, inbox rules that forward mail, messages sent by someone else, or changed payment instructions. Save the original message and any alert details.

2. Secure the account through a known path

Use your company’s normal Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, or email-provider administration process. Reset the password, review recovery methods, and require multifactor authentication where the service supports it. Check which devices, apps, mailbox delegates, forwarding rules, and third-party connections have access. Avoid sharing a new password by text or email.

Ask the administrator to block sign-in while investigating and end active sessions using the provider’s recovery controls. Record suspicious settings, then remove unauthorized recovery methods, forwarding rules, app permissions, and mailbox delegates. A password reset alone is not a complete cleanup. Session revocation timing can vary across services and connected apps. Microsoft’s compromised-account response guide explains the checks for Microsoft 365.

3. Limit the next mistake

Tell the internal team what is known, which messages they should ignore, and where to report anything unusual. If the mailbox sent a suspicious request to customers or vendors, the owner should decide what notice is appropriate. Keep the notice factual and do not add links that could make it look like another phishing message.

4. Review the connected work

Check mail forwarding, shared inbox access, administrator permissions, calendar changes, automatic replies, and sign-ins from unfamiliar devices. Look at whether the account could have been used to reset another service or approve a payment. Coordinate with the people who own those systems before changing settings that affect the business.

5. Recover with a record

When access is stable, document what happened, what was changed, which systems need follow-up, and who owns each action. Review the account-recovery process, training, and access rules so the same confusion does not repeat. A mailbox cleanup is not the same as confirming that every related account and workflow is safe.
