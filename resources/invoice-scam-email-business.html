Invoice Scam Email? What Your Business Should Do

A simple process for checking a suspicious invoice or bank-change email before money, account access, or sensitive information moves.

Published September 25, 20267-minute readBusiness email guidePublished by Odyssey Solutions · Guidance for Greater Houston businesses.

Got an invoice email that feels off? It may use a familiar vendor name, the right logo, or a real invoice number. That still does not make new payment instructions safe to use. The useful first step is to pause the change and verify it through a contact method your office already knows.

Keep it simple: do not change vendor payment details from an email alone. Verify the request through a known contact path, record the result, then follow the office approval process.

1. Pause the payment change

Treat a new bank account, unfamiliar payment method, unexpected invoice, or urgent wire request as a change that needs review. Record the vendor name, invoice number, requested change, sender address, date, and employee who received it. Keep the original message available for the person responsible for verification.

2. Call a number you already have

Use the phone number or vendor portal already in your vendor record. Do not rely only on a number, reply address, or link supplied in the payment-change email. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center advises businesses to use a secondary channel to verify account-information changes.

  • Did an authorized vendor contact request this exact change?
  • Which invoice, purchase order, or account does it affect?
  • What is the effective date?
  • Does the account name match the established vendor record?

3. Compare it with the real vendor record

Check the vendor name, email domain, account manager, invoice number, purchase order, service history, and prior payment details. A request that does not connect to a real order or a known relationship should not move forward until the responsible owner resolves it.

4. Decide who can approve changes

Write down who can add a vendor, change payment details, approve an invoice, release a payment, and contact the bank if something goes wrong. A small office may have one person in several roles. The point is to know when a second review is required and who records it.

5. Protect the email and shared inbox

Payment verification is an office process, but email systems need clear ownership too. Review who can access an accounts-payable mailbox, reset a password, create forwarding rules, or grant administrator access. Require multifactor authentication where the service supports it, especially for email and administrator accounts.

6. Watch for pressure to skip the process

Urgency can be legitimate, but it should not replace verification. Escalate a request that asks staff to keep a change secret, use an unfamiliar payment method, release money before approval, open an unexpected attachment, or sign in through a link to resolve a payment problem. The FTC warns that fake invoices can also be phishing attempts aimed at business data and networks.

7. If something already went wrong

If payment was released, a mailbox rule appeared unexpectedly, or an account may be compromised, preserve the message and note the time. Contact the bank using its established emergency process and notify the people responsible for incident decisions. Involve legal, insurance, privacy, or law-enforcement advisers when the situation requires them. Do not delete evidence or attempt a broad cleanup before the right people understand the scope.

A short process your team can use

  1. Receive the request and pause the change.
  2. Record the vendor, invoice, requested change, and recipient.
  3. Verify through a known contact path.
  4. Compare it with the vendor record.
  5. Obtain the required approval.
  6. Make the change and record the verification.
  7. Escalate unexpected activity to the documented incident contact.
