Is It Safe to Give a Vendor Remote Access?

Sometimes a vendor needs remote access to fix something. Before anyone clicks allow, know who is getting in, what they can reach, and when that access ends.

Published September 25, 20267-minute readVendor access guidePublished by Odyssey Solutions · Guidance for Greater Houston businesses.

Sometimes a software vendor, phone provider, camera installer, imaging company, accountant, or support company really does need remote access to fix something. Before anyone clicks allow, the office should know who is getting in, what they can reach, and when that access ends.

Start with one question: what exact work needs to happen? If the office cannot name the system, vendor contact, internal approver, and end time, access is not ready to approve.

1. Know what the vendor needs to do

Write down the business purpose before access is approved. Is the vendor updating software, troubleshooting phones, checking a network device, supporting imaging, installing equipment, or resolving a cloud-service issue? Then note the exact system, the vendor support contact, the internal requester, the approver, and the expected start and end time.

2. Give access to the right person and system

Use an individual vendor identity when the system supports it. Avoid shared credentials that make it hard to tell who used an account. Limit access to the systems, folders, devices, or administrative functions the vendor needs for the approved work.

Where available, require multifactor authentication for remote and administrative access. CISA recommends beginning with administrators, remote access, email, file storage, and people who handle sensitive information.

3. Put a clock on temporary access

Temporary access needs a maintenance window and an owner who confirms completion. Record when the vendor begins, what work was completed, and when temporary accounts, remote tools, firewall rules, or permissions are removed or reduced.

4. Keep one useful access record

The record does not have to be complicated. It should be available when an office opens, changes vendors, loses an administrator account, or needs to respond to a suspicious event.

  • Vendor name and verified support contact
  • System, device, or service supported
  • Internal business owner and approver
  • Account or remote-access method
  • Start date, review date, and end date
  • Support hours and escalation path
  • Person who can remove or reset access

5. Review access after the work

After the vendor finishes, test the business workflow that depended on the change. Confirm users can sign in, the service works, and the support path is still current. Remove temporary accounts and tools that are no longer needed.

The small-business onboarding and offboarding checklist covers the broader process for regular user access changes.

6. Do not grant unexpected remote access

Treat an unsolicited call, text, pop-up, or email offering remote support as something to verify independently. Use a support number or portal already in the vendor record. Do not rely on a number or link supplied by the unexpected request. If access seems to have been granted unexpectedly, pause work, preserve relevant details, and contact the approved internal and vendor contacts.

Questions to ask before ongoing access

  1. What work requires access, and which systems will be reached?
  2. Who on the vendor team will use the access?
  3. Can it be individual, limited, and time-bound?
  4. Does the system support MFA and activity logging?
  5. Who approves access changes on both sides?
  6. How can the office suspend access during an incident or vendor transition?
  7. What documentation will the office receive after work is complete?

The FTC recommends limiting vendor access to the time and information needed for the work. CISA's small-business vendor guidance also treats logical and physical access as decisions that should be assessed and documented.

Sources

  • CISA: Assess Vendors and Suppliers
  • FTC: Cybersecurity for Small Business
  • CISA: Require Multifactor Authentication

This is general business and technology guidance. Access requirements depend on the system, vendor agreement, business needs, and applicable professional obligations. A checklist alone does not establish security or regulatory compliance.
