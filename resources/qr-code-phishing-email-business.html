QR Code Phishing Emails: How to Spot Them and What to Tell Staff

Scammers put QR codes in emails, attachments and letters to move a phishing link onto a phone. How offices can spot them and what staff should do instead of scanning.

Published November 18, 20263-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

A QR code in an email looks harmless: it is just a square of dots. That is the point. Instead of a link someone could hover over and inspect, the message asks the reader to scan a code with a phone, where the web address is easier to miss.

Federal agencies have warned about malicious QR codes for years, and the FBI warned in January 2026 that attackers were embedding them in targeted phishing emails. Here is how an office can recognize them and what staff should do instead of scanning.

Why QR codes show up in phishing

The FBI has warned that criminals tamper with QR codes so they lead to sites that steal login and financial information. FBI: Cybercriminals tampering with QR codes The FTC has described scammers sending QR codes by email and text with an urgent story, such as a missed delivery or an account problem. FTC: Scammers hide harmful links in QR codes

In a January 2026 alert, the FBI explained that QR codes in emails push people from their work computer to a mobile device, getting around traditional email security controls. It recommends educating employees about scanning unsolicited QR codes from any source, including email, letters, flyers and packaging. FBI FLASH: Malicious QR codes in spearphishing emails

Common signs in a work email

Be suspicious of a QR code that asks you to sign in again, re-enroll multifactor authentication, view a shared document, update payroll or direct deposit details, or confirm a voicemail or fax. Those are tasks that normally happen through a site or app you already use.

Other signs: the code is the only content in an image or PDF attachment, the message pressures you to act within hours, the sender’s address does not match the organization it claims to be from, or the email was unexpected. A real Microsoft 365 or payroll request does not need a QR code.

What staff should do instead

Do not scan it. Report the message using the phishing report button or by forwarding it to whoever handles IT, then delete it. If the request might be real, contact the sender using a phone number or website you already know, not anything printed in the message.

The same rule applies to QR codes on unexpected letters and packages. When a code really is legitimate, the same task can almost always be done by going to the organization’s known website and signing in there.

If someone already scanned it

If a staff member scanned a code and entered a password, treat it as a possible compromised account. Change that password from a known-good computer, sign the account out of other sessions if your system allows it, and tell IT right away so they can check the sign-in history and multifactor settings.

If any payment or banking details were entered, call the bank immediately using the number on the card or statement. Our guide on what to do when a business email account is hacked lists the next steps in order.

Make it part of routine training

Add one QR code example to your next phishing reminder, and tell staff plainly that reporting a suspicious message is always the right call, even if they already clicked or scanned. Fast reports matter more than perfect judgment.
