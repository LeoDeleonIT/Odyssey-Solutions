New Dental Office IT Setup: A Checklist Before You Build Out

Opening a dental office in Houston? Plan cabling, Wi-Fi, firewall, operatory PCs, imaging, software, phones, backups and HIPAA before the walls close.

Published July 20, 2026Updated November 16, 20265-minute readFull-office technology planningPublished by Odyssey Solutions · Greater Houston office buildout and managed IT guidance.

The most expensive IT mistakes in a new dental office get made before anyone from IT shows up. A wall closes without a network drop behind the chair. The server ends up in a closet with no cooling. The internet circuit gets ordered two weeks before opening. None of these are hard to avoid. They just need someone thinking about IT while the floor plan is still on paper.

This checklist is for doctors and office managers opening a new practice or moving into a new space in the Houston area. Use it to ask the right questions of your contractor, your vendors and your IT provider.

Start IT planning before the buildout

Bring IT in when you review the floor plan, not when the furniture arrives. The low-voltage cabling (network, phones, cameras) is usually run after framing and before drywall. Once the walls are closed, adding a drop means cutting and patching.

  1. Walk the floor plan room by room and list every device that needs a network connection or power.
  2. Order internet service as soon as you have an address. New construction can take weeks to get a circuit, and you want time to test it.
  3. Give the cabling contractor a written drop list, not a verbal one.
  4. Pick a network closet or rack location with power, ventilation and a door that locks.

Our new dental office technology timeline lays out what to do at 90, 60 and 30 days before opening.

Network cabling and Wi-Fi

Plan wired drops for every operatory computer, the front desk, the doctor's office, printers, phones and anything imaging related. Wired is more reliable than Wi-Fi for the things that matter during a procedure. Add a spare drop in each operatory. It costs little now and saves a lot later.

Wi-Fi still matters for tablets, patient check-in and staff phones. Ask for access points on the ceiling in the right places, not one router in the closet. Set up separate networks for staff, guests and devices, so a patient on guest Wi-Fi never shares a network with your charting system. Label both ends of every cable and keep the map. Firewall

A business firewall sits between your office and the internet. It controls what gets in, keeps the guest network apart and lets your IT team connect remotely in a controlled way. Do not rely on the box your internet provider drops off. Make sure someone owns the firewall login, the updates and the rules, and that it is written down.

Workstations for every operatory

Each operatory usually needs its own computer for charting, imaging and patient education. Buy them as a matched set from one standard, so a spare can replace any room's machine. Start with what your software vendors require. Open Dental, for example, publishes current computer requirements and recommends a dedicated server to protect data. Our guide to dental office computer standards covers what to write down for front desk, operatory and imaging machines.

X-ray sensors and imaging computers

Imaging is where new offices run into the most trouble on opening week. Before you buy computers, get the requirements from your sensor and imaging software vendor: operating system, USB ports, graphics and where the imaging database will live. Confirm who installs the drivers, who sets up the bridge between imaging and your practice software, and who you call when a sensor stops capturing. Our imaging and sensor setup guide walks through each layer.

Practice management: server or cloud

Decide early whether your practice management software runs on a server in the office or in the cloud, because it changes what you buy.

  • Open Dental runs on a Windows server or workstation in the office. Its computer requirements page lists supported Windows versions.
  • Dentrix is usually installed on an office server, while Ascend by Dentrix is Henry Schein One's cloud-based version.
  • Eaglesoft is supported through Patterson, so get current server and workstation requirements from them. Patterson Dental: Eaglesoft

An on-site server needs a cool, secure spot, a battery backup and a backup plan. A cloud system needs a solid internet connection, and ideally a second one.

Phones and VoIP

Most new dental offices use internet-based phones. That means phone quality depends on your network and internet. Tell your IT provider which phone company you picked, so the firewall and network are set up for voice. Ask the phone provider how 911 calls will show your street address and suite number, and what happens to your phones if the internet goes down.

Backups

Set up backups before the first patient, not after the first scare. You want at least one copy off site, and you want someone to test a restore, not just check that the backup ran. Imaging data counts too. It is often much larger than the practice database and is easy to miss. Odyssey's dental backup and recovery page explains what a tested setup looks like.

HIPAA basics for a new office

Two things to start on before you open:

  • A security risk assessment. HHS requires covered entities to do an accurate and thorough assessment of the risks to electronic patient information. HHS: Guidance on Risk Analysis A new office is the easiest time to start one, because you know exactly what equipment you have. See our guide to HIPAA risk analysis for Texas dental practices.
  • Business associate agreements. Vendors that create, receive, keep or send patient information for you, including cloud and IT providers, need a business associate agreement in place. HHS: Guidance on HIPAA and Cloud Computing Keep a list of every vendor and whether their BAA is signed. Our BAA checklist for dental offices can help.

Before opening day

Run a practice day with staff a few days before patients come in:

  1. Check in a test patient at the front desk.
  2. Take a test image in every operatory and confirm it shows up in the chart.
  3. Print, scan and run a test card payment.
  4. Make and receive a call on every phone.
  5. Unplug the internet for a minute and see what still works.

Write down everything that breaks and who owns the fix. That list is more useful than any spec sheet.
