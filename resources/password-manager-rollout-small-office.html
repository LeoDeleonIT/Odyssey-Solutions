Rolling Out a Password Manager in a Small Office: A Practical Plan

Move shared logins out of spreadsheets and sticky notes. A step-by-step plan for choosing, setting up and rolling out a business password manager in a small office.

Published November 18, 20263-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

Most small offices have a password problem they already know about: the vendor portal login on a sticky note, the shared spreadsheet of accounts, the one person who knows the domain registrar password.

A business password manager fixes that without asking anyone to memorize more. This plan covers how to choose one, set it up and move the team over without disrupting daily work.

Why a password manager

CISA recommends passwords that are long, random and unique for every account: at least 16 characters, or a passphrase of several unrelated words. No one can remember dozens of those, which is why CISA presents a password manager as the practical way to create, store and fill them in. CISA: Use strong passwords

For workplaces, CISA’s guidance goes further and recommends a company-wide password manager paired with multifactor authentication. CISA: Require strong passwords A business plan also gives the office something a personal app cannot: control over shared logins when an employee leaves.

Choose a business plan, not personal accounts

Look for an administrator console, shared folders or vaults that can be limited by role, account recovery handled by an administrator, multifactor authentication for every user and an activity log. Personal plans owned by individual employees leave the business without access when that person leaves.

Decide who owns the account (ideally two administrators, not one) and how the master password and recovery codes for those administrators are stored securely. Healthcare and dental practices should review the vendor’s security documentation as part of their usual vendor review.

Inventory the shared logins first

Before inviting staff, list the accounts the business shares: bank and payroll portals, insurance and clearinghouse portals, practice-management or line-of-business software, the domain registrar and website host, social media, supplier accounts and the Wi-Fi and alarm codes.

Group them into shared folders by role, such as front desk, billing and management, so each person sees only what their job needs. Where a service allows separate user accounts, give people their own login instead of a shared one.

Roll it out in stages

Start with the administrators and one willing team member, move a handful of shared logins and use them for a week. Then hold a short session with the whole team: install the browser extension, turn on multifactor authentication, and show how to save and fill a login.

Set a clear date after which shared passwords live only in the manager, and remove the spreadsheet and sticky notes. Turn off password saving in personal browser profiles on office computers so logins do not end up in two places.

Use it when someone leaves

When an employee leaves, remove their password manager access the same day, then change the shared passwords they could see, starting with banking, payroll and administrator accounts. The manager’s folder list tells you exactly which ones that is. Our employee offboarding access checklist covers the other accounts to close.
