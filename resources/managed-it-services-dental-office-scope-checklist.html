Managed IT for a Dental Office: What the Scope Should Include

What a managed IT agreement for a dental office should cover: help desk, patching, network, backups, security, vendor coordination and a checklist to use.

Published October 28, 20266-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

"Managed IT" can mean very different things from one provider to the next. For one dental office it means unlimited help desk calls, monitoring, backups and on-site visits. For another it means remote monitoring and a phone number, with everything else billed by the hour. Both can be fair arrangements. The problem starts when the practice assumes one and the agreement says the other.

This guide lists what a managed IT scope for a dental practice should address, so an owner or office manager can read a proposal and see what is covered, what is shared and what stays with someone else. If you are still comparing providers, start with our guide on how to choose a dental IT provider in Houston, then use this page to check the paperwork.

Start with what the practice depends on

A useful scope begins with the practice, not the provider's service menu. List the systems that stop patient care when they fail: the practice-management software and its database server, imaging software and sensors, front-desk computers, internet, phones, payment devices and email. Then list who uses them, at how many locations, and during which hours.

CISA's guidance for customers of managed service providers makes the same point for any business that outsources IT: identify the assets most critical to your operations first, then decide which tasks the provider takes over, which you keep and which are shared. CISA: Risk Considerations for MSP Customers

Help desk and on-site support

The scope should say who can request help, how (phone, email, portal), during which hours, and what happens after hours. It should also say how the provider decides what can be fixed remotely and what needs a visit. Failed hardware, cabling, sensor connections and some imaging devices usually need hands on-site.

Ask how priority is set. A sensor that will not capture in an occupied operatory is a different problem from a slow printer in the back office, and the agreement should show that difference.

Workstations, servers and patching

Look for named coverage of:

  • Operatory, front-desk, imaging and back-office computers
  • The server that holds the practice-management database and image folders
  • Operating system and third-party updates, with a stated process for testing and timing
  • Endpoint protection and alert review
  • A current device inventory the practice can see

Patching in a dental office needs scheduling. Updates that restart a computer mid-procedure, or that change a component an imaging bridge depends on, cause real disruption. The scope should say when updates are applied and who checks the clinical workflow afterward.

Network, internet and Wi-Fi

The firewall, switches, wireless access points and internet circuits should be listed, along with who manages the configuration and who holds the admin credentials. Guest Wi-Fi should be separated from clinical and business systems. If the practice has a second internet connection, the scope should say who tests failover and how often. See our dental office network setup guide for what a sound layout includes.

Backups and restore testing

"Backups included" is not enough detail. The scope should name what is backed up, how often, where copies are kept, how long they are retained and how restores are tested.

For Open Dental, the vendor's own backup guidance names both the database and the images folder, recommends at least daily backups, says backed-up data should be encrypted, and says RAID is not a backup. It also recommends verifying backups by restoring them to a separate computer. Open Dental: Backups Other practice-management and imaging systems have their own requirements, so ask the provider to document each one.

Security controls

HHS has published voluntary Cybersecurity Performance Goals for healthcare organizations. The essential goals include multifactor authentication, unique credentials, prompt removal of access for departing staff and basic email security. HHS: HPH Cybersecurity Performance Goals A managed IT scope does not have to use the same wording, but you should be able to see which of these controls the provider implements, which it monitors and which the practice is expected to handle.

Also ask how the provider protects its own access to your systems. CISA's joint advisory on threats to managed service providers recommends multifactor authentication on provider accounts, least privilege, logging of provider activity and clear contract language about security roles. CISA: Protecting Against Cyber Threats to MSPs and their Customers

Vendor coordination

Dental offices depend on many vendors: the practice-management software company, imaging and sensor manufacturers, equipment dealers, the internet provider, the phone provider, payment processors and claims services. A good scope says the IT provider will help isolate where a problem sits and will stay involved with the vendor until it is resolved.

It should also be honest about what stays with each vendor. Database conversions, software licensing, clinical training, product warranties and application-specific fixes normally belong to the vendor. Our dental software support page shows how Odyssey divides that work for the systems it supports.

Staff changes and documentation

New hires, role changes and departures each touch several systems. The scope should say how the practice requests these changes and how quickly access is removed when someone leaves. It should also commit the provider to maintaining documentation the practice can keep: network diagrams, device lists, vendor contacts, admin account owners and backup details. That record matters most on the day you change providers.

What is usually outside the scope

Most agreements exclude or bill separately for new hardware, office build-outs and relocations, major software conversions, after-hours project work and vendor product support. None of that is unusual. It just needs to be written down so there are no surprises.

An IT provider also cannot make a practice HIPAA compliant. If the provider will create, receive, maintain or transmit protected health information on your behalf, HHS rules call for a business associate agreement, and the provider is directly responsible for some safeguards. HHS: Business Associates Policies, risk decisions and legal conclusions remain with the practice and its qualified advisers.

Scope checklist

Before you sign, confirm the agreement answers each of these:

  • Which locations, users and devices are covered?
  • What are the support hours, and what happens after hours?
  • How is priority set for patient-affecting issues?
  • When are updates applied, and who checks clinical software afterward?
  • Who holds admin credentials for the firewall, Microsoft 365, the server and vendor portals?
  • What is backed up, where, for how long, and when was the last restore test?
  • Which security controls does the provider implement and monitor?
  • How does the provider secure and log its own remote access?
  • Which vendors will the provider call, and what stays with each vendor?
  • How are new hires and departures handled?
  • What documentation does the practice receive, and what happens at contract end?
  • Is there a business associate agreement in place?
