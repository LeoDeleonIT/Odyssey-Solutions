Retiring Business Computers? Check the Data Before They Leave

Published October 3, 20264-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions · Guidance for Greater Houston businesses.

A stack of retired computers can look ready for pickup even when nobody has confirmed what is stored on them. The same question applies to external drives, old phones, and a leased copier waiting to be returned.

Before equipment leaves your Houston office, make one person responsible for answering three questions: Have we kept the records we still need? Has the remaining data been handled appropriately? Can we show what happened to each device?

Build a retirement list before booking pickup

Create a record for every item, including its asset number or serial number, former user or location, storage type if known, and intended destination. Identify equipment being reused internally, sold, donated, returned to a vendor, or recycled.

Keep unprocessed equipment in a controlled area. Label its status clearly so that a helpful employee does not hand an unchecked laptop to a pickup driver. A simple sequence works well: awaiting review, approved for sanitization, awaiting verification, and cleared for handoff.

Include devices that stopped working. A computer that will not boot has not demonstrated that its stored information is unreadable.

Preserve what the business still needs

Have the responsible department confirm whether files, records, or configurations must be retained. Resolve any retention requirement or legal hold with the appropriate adviser before authorizing erasure.

For information being moved to another system, verify that the required files are accessible there. Ask the former user or department owner to identify local-only material, such as a report saved outside the usual shared folders. Record the approved destination without copying confidential content into a public disposal spreadsheet.

The FTC recommends a written retention policy that addresses what must be kept, how it is secured, how long it is retained, and how it is disposed of. Its business information-security guide provides a useful starting point.

Match the sanitization method to the device and data

Do not treat a missing desktop file or a successful factory reset as universal proof that a business device is ready to leave. Microsoft specifically says the data-erasure functionality in its consumer “Reset your PC” feature does not meet government and industry erasure standards. See Microsoft's reset guidance.

NIST's media-sanitization guidance distinguishes clearing, purging, and destroying storage. The appropriate choice depends on the information, media, and intended disposition. It is a decision for qualified staff using the applicable technical guidance, not a fixed number of overwrite passes for every drive. See NIST SP 800-88 Revision 2.

Ask the technician or disposal provider to explain the selected method and what evidence it will produce. If a device is damaged or a supported process fails, keep it under control until an appropriate alternative is approved. Do not improvise physical destruction in the office.

Give leased copiers their own check

Some office equipment holds more information than the team expects. The FTC explains that digital-copier hard drives can retain information from documents they copy, print, scan, fax, or email.

Before returning a copier, ask the leasing or servicing company:

  • Does this exact model contain storage holding our documents?
  • Who will handle its data before the device leaves our control?
  • What does the lease permit, and what will the service cost?
  • What record identifies the machine and the work completed?

Do not remove or destroy a leased copier's drive without checking the agreement and technical requirements. The FTC warns that drive removal can affect required firmware and the machine's operation. Its copier data-security guide recommends working with skilled technicians.

Verify the result and close the handoff

Keep the completion evidence with the asset record. NIST provides a sample sanitization certificate covering device identifiers, method, tools, verification, validation, and destination. Its July 16, 2026 FAQ distinguishes checking whether the process completed successfully from deciding whether the result is acceptable for the information involved. See the NIST sanitization FAQ.

For your operational checklist, also record who approved release, who collected the equipment, the handoff date, and any missing or failed items. Reconcile those exceptions before calling the batch complete. Then update the equipment register and have IT review management records, support subscriptions, and device-specific access that may still need closure.

Make retirement part of ordinary IT planning

Add disposal planning to the replacement process instead of waiting until a closet is full. Odyssey's business computer maintenance checklist can help connect that work to the rest of the equipment lifecycle.
