Lost or Stolen Business Laptop? What to Report First

Report a missing work laptop quickly, give IT the right facts, and coordinate account, device and recovery decisions without assuming a remote wipe worked.

Published October 11, 20265-minute readPublished by Odyssey Solutions

If a work laptop is missing, contact your company's IT or security contact promptly from another trusted device. You do not need to prove that it was stolen before reporting it. Give the team what you know and identify what is still uncertain.

An early report lets the business coordinate access decisions, assess the information involved and arrange a safe way for you to keep working. Searching for the laptop and reporting the concern can happen at the same time. Do not delay the report while trying to solve everything yourself.

Send a useful first report

Use the company's established incident channel or a phone number you already trust. If you cannot reach the normal contact, follow the documented escalation route. Avoid responding to an unexpected message claiming that someone needs your password or recovery key to return the computer.

Your initial report should cover:

  • Your name and a safe callback number.
  • The device name, asset tag or serial number, if available elsewhere.
  • When and where you last had it, including the time zone.
  • Whether it was shut down, locked, open or in an unknown state.
  • Whether a phone, security key, removable drive or access badge is also missing.
  • The business applications and categories of information you used on it.

Report uncertainty honestly. “I last used it at 3 p.m., but do not know whether I shut it down” is more useful than a confident guess. Do not include passwords, encryption recovery keys or copies of customer records in the report.

Keep relevant travel, building-security or police-report references with the incident record if applicable. Personal safety comes first; do not confront someone based on a device-location result.

Let IT verify the device's protection

The technical team should match the report to the correct inventory record and check the information available before the loss. That includes management enrollment, recent check-in time and recorded encryption status.

Microsoft describes BitLocker as whole-volume encryption intended to reduce data exposure from lost or stolen Windows devices. Whether a particular laptop had appropriate protection is something to verify from evidence. The presence of a Windows sign-in screen, or a recollection that encryption was enabled, does not complete the incident assessment.

Record the source and age of the available evidence. A last-known device state should not be presented as a current inspection of a computer nobody has recovered. Missing information is a reason for further assessment, not a reason to declare the data safe.

Review accounts as well as the hardware

Tell IT which work accounts and applications were used on the laptop. Include approved remote access, email, cloud files and browser-based business systems. The incident lead can coordinate with the owners of those services and decide which access restrictions or account-recovery steps are appropriate.

Microsoft's emergency access-revocation guidance explains that access can persist for a period after revocation begins. An application may also manage its own session, which needs its own handling. A single sign-out or password-change action should not be treated as proof that every application session has ended.

Have authorized administrators document the actions taken, their timing and the checks still outstanding. Avoid making unrelated tenant-wide changes during a device incident. The goal is a coordinated response based on the affected accounts and actual evidence.

Treat a remote wipe as a consequential decision

A managed device may support remote actions, but availability depends on its platform, enrollment and configuration. Microsoft's Intune wipe documentation describes a factory-reset action that can remove personal and organizational data, with different options and behavior by platform.

That makes wiping a decision for the authorized incident lead and administrator. They need to verify the target device, the intended effect, required approval and any evidence-preservation needs. An employee should not experiment with reset options or remove the device from management while the response is being coordinated.

Execution also matters. Microsoft notes that the remote reset or managed-app wipe processes described in its access-revocation guidance require the device to connect to the internet. A request submitted to a console is not proof that the missing computer received or completed it. Record the actual result and keep unresolved actions visible.

Assess the information and restore work safely

Help the incident lead identify the categories of data that may have been stored locally or accessible through signed-in applications. Be specific about the work involved without copying sensitive material into an ordinary ticket.

If customer, employee, financial or patient information may be involved, involve the organization's responsible privacy/security lead and counsel. They can assess the facts and any reporting duties. Avoid promising that encryption or a wipe request automatically settles those questions.

For a replacement device, have IT restore the approved working environment and check the files needed for the next business day. Flag recent work that may not have reached an approved backup or cloud location. Do not move company records to a personal computer as an improvised workaround.

If the original laptop is found, report that promptly and follow IT's instructions before returning it to use. Close the incident only after the responsible team has reviewed the remaining access, device and recovery questions.

Make the reporting route easy to find

Keep the incident contact available somewhere other than the laptop itself. Odyssey's business IT services include device support, access controls and security coordination.
